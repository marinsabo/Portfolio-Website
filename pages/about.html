About me

One night in Morocco, I slept in a tent in the middle of the Sahara. We got there on camels, and when the sun went down there was nothing left but sand, silence and more stars than I had ever seen. I lay there looking at that vast sky, fascinated, and felt something I rarely feel: complete peace. In that quietness I felt in place, connected to myself and to God. I’ve been to 18 countries, but that night is the one I keep coming back to.

It makes sense that I found that feeling outside. Where I grew up, we spent our days on the playground from sunrise to sunset, and, if our parents let us, until dusk. Video games never made sense to us: why play a simulation of something you can do in real life? You could hardly find me indoors. The world seemed mine to explore, and I never really stopped believing that.

Somewhere along the way I noticed that most things, left alone, slowly run down. Bodies get weaker, minds get lazier, days start to look the same. Routine becomes autopilot, comfort quietly becomes the goal, and before you know it, life is something that happens to you. I don’t want that kind of life. Most of what I do, I do to fight that slow decay. If I’m honest, that’s also my biggest fear: waking up one day and realising the autopilot got me too, that I became ordinary.

Sport was the first way I fought it. Teachers used to tell me I had too much energy and needed to channel it. So I did: 7 years of judo, 3 of basketball, 2 of kickboxing, and now the gym, running and any sport I can play. Running matters most. While my body works, my mind finally goes quiet, a little like that night in the desert. It has carried me through several half-marathons, and right now I’m preparing for a Spartan Race.

Code came later, and there’s no plot twist. I chose a computing high school only because it seemed a fraction more interesting than Latin and art history. At 16 I wrote my first HTML and CSS, and I was hooked by the idea that anyone could show the world who they are. Helping others do that became my first work: freelancing. Over time I found that what I care about most is engineering, doing things the right, clean way. I’ve always been a minimalist, and building something simple and intentional feels like the same fight against entropy, just in a different form.

I try to live the same way. Since reading Cal Newport’s Digital Minimalism, I use social media only for very specific purposes, keep few apps on my phone and barely touch it. I have strict rules for how I use AI. It isn’t about rejecting technology. It’s about making sure I use it, not the other way around.

The same goes for people. I can’t stand passivity, in myself or around me, and maybe that’s why I usually end up leading. I make the ambitious plan and then jump into the trenches with everyone else. Not everyone likes that. I’ve been told many times that I hold myself too high, in how I stand, move and speak, and that when I talk about my experience it sounds like bragging. Maybe it does. I believe I’m special, and I don’t hide it. But I’ve never let anyone down, and I’ve come to understand that when someone sees me do what they don’t yet have the courage to do, it can feel like I’m pointing at their weakness. I’m not. I just believe people are capable of much more than autopilot, and if I make someone feel something, even annoyance at me, that’s better than a flat line.

I don’t expect everyone to understand me, and I’ve made peace with that. Being different has a price, and I’d rather pay it than live an ordinary life. If any of this resonates with you, or if you think I’m completely wrong, I’d love to talk. Big questions are my favourite kind: why we live the way we do, what makes a life worth living, what we’re here for. You can reach me through the contact page, and my education and career are in my CV.
